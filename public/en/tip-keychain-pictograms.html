Keychain pictograms: how to use them with a nonverbal student

November 17, 2026 · 6 min read · By Mélodie Fréchette, Filodie

A student who does not speak, or speaks very little, still has a lot to say: it hurts, I need a break, I want to play. Keychain pictograms give them pictures that are always at hand, and give the adult a simple way to show what comes next.

What is a keychain pictogram?

They are small 6 x 6 cm cards, printed, laminated and punched, then slipped onto a ring. The ring clips onto a belt, a bag or a belt loop, with a carabiner or a badge reel. Each card shows a single idea, with a clear picture and a written word.

The adult uses them to give an instruction without many words (“Wait your turn”, “Tidy up”). The student uses them to express a need, a feeling or a choice, by touching the card or handing it over.

Who are they for?

  • Nonverbal or minimally verbal students, autistic or not.
  • Students who speak but lose their words when emotions run high.
  • Students who understand what they see better than what they hear, for example with an intellectual disability, a language disorder or while learning English.
  • The adults who support them: special care counsellors, teachers, daycare staff, parents.

Start with a single card

  1. Choose the most useful card right now. Often Bathroom, Break or Help: a need that comes up every day.
  2. Show the card and say the word. Every time the situation comes up, the adult shows the card and simply says the word.
  3. Wait a few seconds. Give the student time to touch the card or hand it over.
  4. Respond right away. When the student uses the card, grant the request as soon as possible. That is how they learn that the picture works.
  5. Add one card at a time. Once the first card is used well, add another one.

One colour per category

Each card’s border has the colour of its category: emotions in purple, needs in blue, expected behaviours in green, what not to do in red, routines in orange, and so on. The student and the adult find the right card at a glance, even when the ring is full.

There is no need to carry everything at once. For a trip to the grocery store, put only the cards for the outing on the ring, in the order they will happen. For a dentist appointment, only the cards for the visit.

Putting the ring together

  1. Print at 100% (actual size), without “Fit to page”.
  2. Cut out, then laminate, leaving at least 3 mm between cards.
  3. Trim again, keeping a plastic edge so the card stays waterproof.
  4. Punch at the round mark in the corner and slip onto a 2.5 cm ring.

Do pictures stop children from talking?

Parents often worry about this. Research does not show that visual communication supports hold back speech; for many children, speech even improves a little (Millar et al., 2006). The picture does not replace the word: always say the word while showing the card. Visual supports are also among the recognized practices for autistic students (Hume et al., 2021).

Using the cards everywhere

  • At school: every adult who works with the student uses the same cards, with the same words.
  • At home: a copy of the ring for the family helps the student use their cards outside the classroom.
  • On the wall: the same cards, enlarged, become a communication board or a posted routine.
Tip for the adult

Always keep a “Break” card on the ring, even at the start. Being able to ask for a break is often the card that prevents the most meltdowns.

References: Hume, K. et al. (2021). Evidence-based practices for children, youth, and young adults with autism: Third generation review. Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 51(11), 4013-4032. Millar, D. C., Light, J. C., & Schlosser, R. W. (2006). The impact of augmentative and alternative communication intervention on the speech production of individuals with developmental disabilities: A research review. Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research, 49(2), 248-264.
